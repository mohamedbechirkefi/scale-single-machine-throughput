\section{Abstract}\label{abstract}

\textbf{Background.} Advice on making a local web service faster usually
names caching, indexing, batching or admission control, but the size of
each remedy depends on the system it is applied to. \textbf{Question.}
On one fixed machine and workload, which of these four changes, each
applied alone to a simple baseline, changes peak successful throughput
the most? \textbf{Method.} A standard-library Python HTTP server backed
by SQLite (200,000 rows, Zipf-distributed keys, 2 \% writes) was run in
five arms: a full-scan, thread-per-connection baseline and four
one-factor changes. A protocol frozen by the owner before any
confirmatory data (not registered with an external registry) fixed the
primary metric (peak goodput over a closed-loop concurrency sweep from 1
to 512 connections), the within-block ratio to the baseline, percentile
bootstrap intervals with a Bonferroni correction, and a rule that
excludes cells whose goodput exceeds half the measured load-generator
ceiling. Ten paired blocks (50 accepted runs; 12 further runs excluded
by the idle-load probe and re-attempted) ran on a quiet machine.
\textbf{Results.} Read batching raised peak goodput by a factor of 9.01
(98.75 \% interval 8.92 to 9.09) and the cache by 2.34 (2.25 to 2.44).
Bounded concurrency had no meaningful effect on peak goodput (1.015,
1.000 to 1.030), because the baseline peaks at four connections, below
the admission limit. The index arm matched the measurement ceiling
(median 28,131 requests per second against a 27,919 ceiling) in every
block and was excluded by the frozen rule, so the question of which
factor dominates is unresolved. \textbf{Conclusion.} Under this design
the point estimates order batching above caching and both above
admission control for peak goodput; exploratory pairwise intervals
separate them, but the frozen analysis has no pairwise test. Both
winning effects are conditional on the full-scan baseline, the size of
the batching advantage depends on the summary metric, and the open-loop
and workload sensitivity experiments did not run.

\section{1. Introduction}\label{introduction}

When a service is too slow, the usual remedies are a cache, an index,
batching or a limit on concurrent work. Which one helps most is a
question about a particular system, not about the remedies in general:
the answer depends on where the time goes. This study asks the question
for one small, fully specified system and answers it under a protocol
that was frozen before any confirmatory data existed.

\textbf{Research question.} On one fixed machine (Apple M1 Pro, 10
cores, 16 GB, macOS 15.3.1) and one fixed local HTTP workload served by
one Python process backed by SQLite, which single architecture change,
applied one factor at a time to a simple baseline, produces the largest
change in peak successful throughput: an in-process cache (A1), a
database index (A2), read batching (A3) or bounded concurrency (A4)?

\textbf{Hypotheses.} For each treatment arm j, H0\_j: R\_j = 1, against
the nondirectional H1\_j: R\_j differs from 1, where R\_j is the
within-block ratio of the arm's peak goodput to the baseline's (section
3). The directional expectations were recorded for interpretation only:
R\_A1, R\_A2 and R\_A3 above 1, A4 indeterminate, and A2 largest
\textbf{by design}, because the baseline answers every lookup with a
full scan (protocol amendment AM-2, approved by the owner before the
freeze).

\textbf{Contributions.} The contributions are deliberately narrow. No
novelty is claimed for the mechanisms, which are textbook, and no claim
is made that a comparable study does not exist; the literature search
was targeted, not systematic (section 2).

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  A reproducible single-machine harness with a frozen protocol, a freeze
  guard in the command-line tool, pre-stated rules for the values set
  after the freeze, and a validated load generator (EXP-001).
\item
  Paired effect sizes with bootstrap intervals for three of the four
  factors (EXP-002), and the reasons the fourth could not be estimated
  under the frozen rule.
\item
  An exploratory re-analysis of the recorded runs that explains the null
  result for bounded concurrency, shows how much the batching advantage
  depends on the summary metric, and documents the error composition at
  high concurrency.
\item
  A complete record of what did not run (EXP-003, EXP-004) and why.
\end{enumerate}

\section{2. Related work}\label{related-work}

This is a targeted, non-systematic review of thirteen sources
(\texttt{research/literature\_review.md}). Each citation below states
how much of the source was actually read.

\textbf{Measurement methodology.} Mytkowicz et al.~{[}mytkowicz2009;
abstract and author page read{]} show that setup choices that look
innocuous can bias performance measurements; this motivated randomising
arm order within blocks and checking for a time trend. Kalibera and
Jones {[}kalibera2013; full text of the accepted manuscript read{]}
recommend spending repetitions at the level of the experiment that
carries the most variance and reporting effect sizes with confidence
intervals; here the replicate is a fresh server process on a freshly
built database. Their later note {[}kalibera2020; full text obtained
from arXiv, claim checked against the abstract and introduction{]}
treats ratios with intervals as the reporting unit, which matches the
R\_j effect used here.

\textbf{Load models.} Schroeder, Wierman and Harchol-Balter
{[}schroeder2006; full text obtained, claim checked against the abstract
and introduction{]} show that closed-loop and open-loop load generators
can give very different response times at the same load, and that
conclusions about scheduling can change between the two models. Tene's
wrk2 documentation {[}tene-wrk2; project page read{]} describes
coordinated omission in closed-loop latency measurement and the remedy
of measuring from the intended send time. Both motivate the open-loop
sensitivity experiment EXP-003, which this study planned but did not
run.

\textbf{Saturation.} Little's result {[}little1961; bibliographic
metadata only, full text not read{]} is the queueing identity L = λW,
named in its title; it is used here only as a consistency check of the
closed-loop data. Gunther's Universal Scalability Law {[}gunther2008,
gunther2011; full texts obtained from arXiv, claim checked against the
definition of the Universal Scalability Law in each{]} describes
throughput against concurrency with contention and coherency terms; it
is mentioned as a possible descriptive summary and was not fitted. Dean
and Barroso {[}dean2013; abstract read{]} motivate reporting tail
latency, not only means.

\textbf{Relation to this study.} The study replicates no prior
experiment. It applies the measurement advice above to a small
comparison of four standard remedies, and its contribution is the
protocol discipline and the complete reporting of a bounded result, not
a new mechanism.

\section{3. Background and formal
model}\label{background-and-formal-model}

\subsection{3.1 Notation and
definitions}\label{notation-and-definitions}

For one run, one concurrency level c and a measurement window of length
T = 15 s:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Goodput} G(c) = (number of HTTP 200 responses whose body
  passes validation) / T, in requests per second.
\item
  \textbf{Peak goodput} G* = max over c of G(c), with ties resolved to
  the smallest c.
\item
  \textbf{Effect} R\_j = G*\_j / G*\_A0 within a block, summarised by
  the geometric mean over blocks, exp(mean of log R\_j).
\item
  \textbf{Generator-limited cell.} A level whose goodput exceeds 50 \%
  of the generator ceiling C\_gen, or whose generator CPU exceeds 85 \%
  of its allotted cores (protocol section 4).
\item
  \textbf{Saturation concurrency} c\_sat = the smallest c with G(c) at
  least 0.95 G*.
\end{itemize}

The closed loop has zero think time, so the mean number of requests in
flight equals c, and Little's identity gives c = X · W for throughput X
and mean response time W.

\subsection{3.2 Workload and system
model}\label{workload-and-system-model}

One server process (Python 3.12, standard library \texttt{http.server}
and \texttt{sqlite3}, HTTP/1.1 keep-alive, 127.0.0.1 only) serves a
synthetic table of N = 200,000 rows with a 256-byte payload, rebuilt
from a fixed seed for every run (WAL mode). 98 \% of requests are reads
\texttt{GET\ /item?sku=...} and 2 \% are price writes. Keys follow a
Zipf distribution with exponent 0.99 over a seeded permutation; the
request sequence of a block is identical across its five arms. The arms

\section{4. Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{4.1 Design and arms}\label{design-and-arms}

The design is one factor at a time against the baseline: each treatment
arm differs from A0 in exactly one respect, and interactions are not
estimated. The experimental unit is a run: a fresh server on a fresh
database, one arm and one full concurrency sweep. A block executes all
five arms in a seeded random order; blocks are the independent
replicates, and the arms within a block are paired by request sequence
and adjacency in time.

\subsection{4.2 Workload, data and
controls}\label{workload-data-and-controls}

The data are synthetic (common decision C1). Before any confirmatory
run, the test suite checked response equivalence across arms, cache
invalidation (including under concurrent readers), the absence of lost
writes under a seeded concurrent write sequence, and load-generator
limits. A run starts only if the system-wide CPU over a 1 s idle probe
is below 15 \%; the load average, power source, OS, CPU, package
versions, git revision and config hash are recorded per run.

\subsection{4.3 Load generation and
measurement}\label{load-generation-and-measurement}

A separate generator process on the same machine drives keep-alive
connections and timestamps with a monotonic clock. EXP-002 uses a closed
loop with c in \{1, 2, 4, \ldots, 512\}, 5 s warmup and a 15 s
measurement window per level. EXP-001 measured the generator ceiling on
a null endpoint (\texttt{GET\ /null}, no database work) at c in \{1, 8,
32\}, cross-checked each level against ApacheBench 2.3, and set C\_gen
by the pre-stated rule AM-5: the median of the five run ceilings.

\subsection{4.4 Seeds, replication and resource
budget}\label{seeds-replication-and-resource-budget}

The sweep top c\_max and the number of blocks n were left open at the
freeze and set by a pre-stated rule from a quiet-machine pilot (protocol
section 7.1, rule version pilot-rule-1.0). The rule reads only the
concurrency at which A1 and A3 peak and the between-block spread of log
R, never the size, sign or ranking of an effect. The pilot (25 runs, 5
blocks, seed 20261126) gave A1 peaks at c = 4 in every block and A3
peaks at c = 128 or 256, so c\_max = 512; the largest between-block SD
of log R was 0.0208 (A1), below the 0.07 trigger, so n = 10. The EXP-002
study seed is 20261124. EXP-002 took 10540.9 s of machine time at zero
cost.

\subsection{4.5 Statistical analysis}\label{statistical-analysis}

The frozen analysis (analysis-1.0, \texttt{src/scale/analysis.py}) was
written and tested before any confirmatory output existed (decision
D-012). Per block it computes log R\_j; the estimate is the
back-transformed mean; intervals are percentile bootstrap over blocks
with 10,000 resamples and seed 20261124, at 95 \% and at the
Bonferroni-adjusted 98.75 \% level for four comparisons. H0\_j is
rejected when the 98.75 \% interval excludes 1. ``No meaningful effect''
requires the whole 98.75 \% interval inside {[}0.95, 1.05{]}; otherwise
a non-rejection is inconclusive. A dominance claim requires every 98.75
\% interval of R\_t / R\_k for the top arm t to exclude 1, and is
attempted only if all four treatment arms enter the comparison.

Two readings of the frozen text were fixed in D-012 before data: a run's
confirmatory G* is undefined when the level attaining it is
generator-limited, and an arm enters the comparison only if its G* and
the baseline's are defined in every block, because dropping single
blocks would select blocks by outcome. The owner has not yet reviewed
D-012.

The frozen sensitivity analyses are S3 (G* replaced by the mean goodput
over c in \{16, 32, 64\}) and a drift check (least squares of log G* on
arm indicators plus run order, and separately plus position in block,
with a 2,000-resample block bootstrap).

reproducibility}\label{protocol-registration-and-reproducibility}

The protocol and the four experiment protocols were frozen by the owner

\subsection{4.7 Exploratory versus confirmatory
work}\label{exploratory-versus-confirmatory-work}

Confirmatory: EXP-001 and EXP-002 as frozen. Exploratory, and labelled

and 0.173).

\section{6. Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{6.1 Mechanisms supported by the
evidence}\label{mechanisms-supported-by-the-evidence}

\textbf{The baseline is bound by parallel full scans.} At c = 1 the
baseline spends about 18.3 ms of server CPU per validated response (X2),
consistent with one full scan per lookup; the query plan for its lookup
is a full table scan (X13, \texttt{SCAN\ items}). Server CPU above one
core shows that scans ran in parallel (why the interpreter permits this
was not examined): goodput rises to c = 4, where server CPU is about 3.8
cores, and then falls, while the CPU cost per response rises to between
about 160 and 178 ms from c = 16 with all ten cores busy and a
system-time share of 0.89. The extra work per request under
oversubscription is consistent with contention in the operating system
or memory system, but this study did not isolate it.

\textbf{Batching wins by sharing scans.} A3's mean batch size was 15.97.
With no index, SQLite 3.43.1 plans the batched \texttt{IN\ (...)} query
as one full scan (X13), so one scan serves a whole batch, so the
per-response CPU cost falls to about 1.5 ms at the plateau while server
CPU stays near 1.5 cores. The ninefold gain is therefore an amortisation
of the baseline's full scan. It is a property of batching
\textbf{against a full-scan baseline}, in the same sense as the index
result, and it should not be read as a general value of batching.

\textbf{The cache gain is close to its hit-ratio bound.} With a median
hit ratio of 0.59 the cache can at most remove 59 \% of the scans, a
bound of 1 / (1 - 0.59) = 2.44; the observed 2.337 is close to it. The
hit ratio is counted over the whole run, warmups and all levels
included, not at the peak level, so the comparison is approximate. The
hit ratio depends on the skew, the capacity and the warmup, which were
fixed and not varied.

\textbf{The A4 null is structural.} Both A4 and the baseline peak at c =
4, below the admission limit W = 8, where the limiter does not engage
(X6). Equal peaks are then expected, and the confirmatory ``no
meaningful effect'' says only that the limiter costs nothing at the
baseline's optimum. At the unadjusted 95 \% level the interval (1.003 to
1.027) excludes 1, so a small positive difference at c = 4 cannot be
ruled out; it lies inside the practical band either way. Above the
optimum the limiter does help: it holds goodput about 26 \% above the
baseline at c = 16 and, under S3, 16 \% over c in \{16, 32, 64\}, at the
price of rejecting almost every request from c = 32.

\subsection{6.3 Implications and practical
significance}\label{implications-and-practical-significance}

Within its boundary the study gives a concrete ordering: for this server
and workload, sharing a full scan across requests (A3) raised peak
goodput much more than caching the hot keys (A1), and limiting
concurrency (A4) did not change the peak. The lesson that the data
support is narrow and specific to this system: the remedies that won are
the ones that remove or share its dominant cost, the full scan. Whether
this pattern holds for other systems is not tested here. The index,
which removes the scan outright, was too fast for the measurement
harness; its true advantage over the others could not be measured by
this design.

\subsection{6.4 Relation to prior work}\label{relation-to-prior-work}

The result illustrates two points from the methodology literature. The
dependence of the A3 advantage on the summary metric (3.85 under G*,
1.58 under a closed-loop latency bound) is the kind of conclusion that
Schroeder et al.~warn can change with the load model, and the open-loop
experiment that would test it did not run. The narrow intervals reflect
the replication strategy recommended by Kalibera and Jones, but they
describe variation between process restarts within one session on one
machine (section 7).

\section{7. Limitations and threats to
validity}\label{limitations-and-threats-to-validity}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{One machine, one session.} All confirmatory runs were made on
  one laptop in one window of about three hours. The intervals describe
  variation between process restarts within that window, not between
  days, machines or operating-system states; they are narrower than a
  reproduction elsewhere would be.
\item
  \textbf{One implementation and workload.} Python standard library,
  SQLite 3.43.1, N = 200,000, Zipf 0.99, 2 \% writes. Nothing transfers
  to other implementations, to production traffic or to other hardware.
\item
  \textbf{The baseline decides the winners.} The full-scan baseline
  makes the index dominant by design (AM-2) and makes batching an
  amortisation of scans. A baseline with an index might reorder A1, A3
  and A4; this was not tested.
\item
  \textbf{A2 has no confirmatory estimate.} The exclusion rests on the
  D-012 reading, pending the owner; the exploratory ratios near 247 and
  91 measure the harness ceiling or a degraded region, not the index
  alone.
\item
  \textbf{No pairwise test in the frozen analysis.} The order A3
  \textgreater{} A1 \textgreater{} A4 is a ranking of point estimates in
  the confirmatory analysis; the pairwise intervals of section 5.5 are
  exploratory.
\item
  \textbf{Metric dependence.} The size of the A3 and A4 effects changes
  with the summary statistic (G*, S3, closed-loop latency bound).
\item
  \textbf{A4 null is structural} (section 6.1) and concerns peak goodput
  only.
\item
  \textbf{Errors not fully diagnosed.} The server logs HTTP 500 without
  the exception type; transport errors at c of 256 and above are
  unclassified. The confirmatory runs validate every read body but do
  not re-check the final database state; write correctness rests on the
  pre-run tests.
\item
  \textbf{Re-attempted cells.} Seven cells of blocks 8 and 9 ran at the
  end of the sequence, paired by request sequence but not in time;
  leaving them out changes no verdict (X5).
\item
  \textbf{Unrun sensitivity experiments.} EXP-003 and EXP-004 did not
  run (sections 5.3 and 5.4).
\item
  \textbf{Shared CPU and scheduler.} Generator and server share the
  cores; the placement on performance or efficiency cores is not
  controllable.
\item
  \textbf{Pilot effect sizes were visible before EXP-002.} The
  pre-stated rule did not use them, and the analysis was committed
  before EXP-002 output (C4 disclosure in the freeze record).
\item
  \textbf{Post hoc exploratory checks.} The checks X1 to X13 were chosen
  at M4, after the confirmatory results were known; X12 in particular
  was added after the per-level latencies had been inspected. They are
  hypothesis-generating only.
\item
  \textbf{AI assistance.} The analysis readings of D-012 and every
  exploratory check were written by an AI assistant; they are internal
  quality control, not independent review.
\end{enumerate}

\section{8. Ethics and responsible
research}\label{ethics-and-responsible-research}

The study uses synthetic data only, involves no human participants and
no personal data, and sends no traffic beyond the local loopback
interface. It makes no claim about any real service, product or
organisation. No ethics approval was sought or needed, and none is
claimed.

\section{9. Conclusion and future
work}\label{conclusion-and-future-work}

On this machine and workload, read batching raised peak goodput about
ninefold and an LRU cache about 2.3-fold over a full-scan baseline, and
bounded concurrency left the peak unchanged because the baseline peaks
below the admission limit. The index reached the measurement ceiling,
and under the frozen rule the question of which factor dominates is
unresolved. The ordering of batching above caching holds under every
summary used here, but its size is metric-dependent.

Feasible extensions, each needing an owner decision: set EXP-003's four
open parameters and run the open-loop test; decide on EXP-004; and, as a
new study rather than an amendment, raise the harness ceiling (for
example with a compiled generator on separate cores) so that the index
can be measured, and add an indexed baseline.
